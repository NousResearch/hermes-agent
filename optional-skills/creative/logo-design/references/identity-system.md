# From Logo to Identity System

A logo is the foundation; the identity system is the framing built on it. The audience never meets a logo in a
void — always on a card, a van, a screen, a building, next to photography and words. Read this when the user needs
more than a mark: a kit of parts, sub-brands, patterns, a dynamic identity, motion, or guidelines.

## 1. Principles of a system

- **Consistency of concept** — every element (visual and verbal) identifies the organisation to its audiences.
  Consistency is central to branding; it doesn't preclude creativity. A predictable, lifeless system fails; power,
  clarity and freshness must accompany consistency.
- **Clarity of message** — every element supports the logo and the intended perception; nothing competes with it.
- **Derived, not duplicated** — the system grows *from* the mark (its angles, radii, colours, a fragment) but
  complements rather than copies it.
- **Accommodate the user** — understand who will use the system (in-house designers, agencies, printers, franchisees,
  developers) and how; make it customisable within constraints.
- **Flexibility** — other creatives will use it; plan enough variables to keep it fresh, and make it damage-proof so
  imperfect use still looks on-brand. At some point the original designer must let go; good rules make that safe.

## 2. The kit of parts (checklist)

**Logo & variations**
- Primary mark/signature (symbol + wordmark) and variations: horizontal, stacked, symbol-only, wordmark-only.
- Size variants: a simplified small-size version; redrawn details for very large use if needed.
- Media variants: e.g. heavier line weights for broadcast/screen, single-colour for embroidery.
- Brand extensions and product logos; relationship with partner or trademark symbols.

**Colour palettes**
- Primary colour(s) and acceptable alternatives; black/white usage; can it be reversed, or reversed only out of a
  containing shape? Exact Pantone, CMYK, RGB, HEX values. Secondary/accent palette.

**Typography**
- Primary and secondary families, weights, italics; web/app fallbacks; rules for headlines vs body.

**Taglines and modifying copy**
- Primary and alternative taglines, typeface, size ratio to the logo, legal lines, ™/® usage.

**Imagery**
- Photography/illustration style, when and how used, ratio and proximity to the logo, image library access.

**Containing shapes / graphic devices**
- Is the logo ever placed in a shape? Always or only in some contexts? Does the shape change? Can it hold other
  content?

**Patterns, icons, textures**
- Brand pattern(s), icon set built on the logo's geometry, supporting graphic elements.

**Motion and sound** (screen, broadcast, product)
- How the logo moves and resolves; how it interacts with other elements; sonic signature if relevant.

**Voice**
- Editorial tone — part of the identity even though it isn't visual.

## 3. Logo systems and sub-brands

- **Branded house**: one master mark; sub-brands expressed by descriptor text, colour, or a consistent modifier.
  Maximum equity transfer, least flexibility.
- **Endorsed brands**: each sub-brand has its own mark with a visible link to the parent ("by Parent"), or shares a
  structural element (frame, typeface, icon grid).
- **House of brands**: independent marks; the parent is invisible to consumers.
- Decide on hierarchy deliberately: is association with the parent an advantage or a liability for the product?
  If association would position the product badly, that's a strategy problem to solve before any design.
- Keep repeated elements truly consistent (same typeface, same symbol proportions, same spacing rules); vary one
  dimension at a time (colour *or* icon *or* descriptor).

## 4. Static vs. changeable (dynamic) identities

- A logo must provide a cohesive voice, but it need not be immutable. Screen media expects logos to move, react and
  sometimes change. Someone will make it spin — better that the designer defines how.
- Dynamic identity patterns:
  - **Fixed frame, changing content** — a container or grid that holds changing images, colours or patterns.
  - **Fixed type, changing symbol** — consistent letterforms and colour; the accompanying shape varies per
    application.
  - **Approved sets** — e.g. a square of colour from 12 approved colours with the name set in one of 12 approved
    typefaces; users combine freely within the sets.
  - **Generative** — a rule (data, date, location, user input) that produces variants.
  - **Many logos, one voice** — several marks used discreetly for sophisticated audiences who are weary of loud
    branding; recognition comes from consistent attitude rather than one symbol.
- Whatever changes, keep at least two anchors constant (e.g. name typography + colour, or frame + position).
- The action of the logo must reinforce the concept; motion for its own sake dilutes it.

## 5. Patterns and graphic devices

- A pattern is often the most important branding element after the logo. It must complement the mark without
  duplicating it and work from a business card to an aircraft, in signage and interiors.
- Build from components of the mark when possible (a fragment, the construction grid, the key angle); if the mark
  offers nothing, develop a new visual language on par with or deliberately contrasting the logo.
- Grid choice drives character: square (versatile, common), triangular, hexagonal, rotated; see
  `visual-techniques.md` §13.
- Graphic devices (containers, outlines, bands, frames) solve visibility on busy backgrounds and create recognisable
  layouts; keep their proportions in sync with the logo's.

## 6. Motion and sound

- Define: entrance (how it builds), resolve (final static state, which must equal the master), exit, loop; duration
  (logo stings are typically 1–3 s); easing character matching the brand (snappy vs. gentle).
- Build motion from the logo's construction (strokes drawing on, modules assembling, a rotation that reveals a
  negative shape).
- Sound signature for broadcast/apps: short, ownable, consistent; specify when it plays.

## 7. Standards manual / brand guidelines

Useful to the widest group of users (in-house designers, agencies, PR, marketing, printers, signage fabricators,
packaging, vehicle and uniform suppliers, purchasing). Written in plain language for non-designers. Prefer friendly
rules and more "do"s than "don't"s.

**Suggested anatomy** (scale down for a start-up; a one-page sheet can be enough):
1. **Introduction** — a short letter from leadership (drives buy-in), the brand idea, how to use the manual.
2. **Logo** — the mark and its rationale; versions and lockups; construction; **clear space** (defined by an element
   of the logo, e.g. the height of the symbol's dot or the cap height, so it scales — never a fixed distance);
   **minimum sizes** (px and mm); placement; logo on backgrounds (black on white, white on black, colour, photo);
   **misuse** examples (stretching, recolouring, rotating, adding effects, rearranging lockups, outlining, placing on
   busy backgrounds without a device).
3. **Colour** — primary and secondary palettes with all colour codes; proportions; approved logo/background pairs.
4. **Typography** — families, hierarchy, spacing; kerning of the wordmark at large sizes if relevant.
5. **Imagery, iconography, patterns, graphic devices** — rules and examples.
6. **Grids and layouts** — margins and gutters chosen deliberately (square-ish modules are the most versatile).
7. **Applications** — stationery (business cards: clean front with name, title, contact; pattern or colour on the back;
   letterhead: nothing printed on the back), digital (web, social avatars, email signatures), signage, vehicles,
   uniforms, packaging, advertising, merchandise.
8. **Motion and sound** if relevant.
9. **Contacts and files** — who to ask, where the master files live.

## 8. Rollout and stewardship

- **Rollout**: plan the order of applications (digital first is cheap; signage and vehicles are expensive), supervise
  early production (printing, fabrication), and coordinate with in-house teams and outside agencies.
- **Asset management**: one source of truth for master files, named clearly, accessible to all users.
- **Audit periodically** (monthly → quarterly → yearly after launch): review materials for consistency, production
  quality and supplier compliance; interview users about friction; adapt the system to new needs; track changes in
  the competitive landscape; report findings and celebrate good applications. A system that is never reviewed
  decays within a year.
