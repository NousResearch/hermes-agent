# Logo Design Principles

The foundational thinking behind every decision in this skill. Read this when you need to justify a
direction, resolve a disagreement, critique a mark, or when a design "feels off" and you need a lens.

## 1. What a logo is (and is not)

- **A logo is an identifier, not an explanation.** People are known by a name, not by a paragraph
  describing where they live and what they do. A logo is a signpost: it points to the organisation and
  hints at its attitude. It does not need to show the product, the service, the city and the founding
  story at once.
- **A logo is not an illustration.** An illustration explains text. A logo is a shortcut that must be
  recognised in a glance and remembered afterwards.
- **Logo ≠ identity ≠ brand.**
  - *Logo*: the mark itself (symbol, wordmark, or both).
  - *Identity*: the logo plus its visual system — typefaces, colours, imagery, patterns, tone of voice.
  - *Brand*: the perception people hold about the organisation. It is formed by the audience from every
    touchpoint — products, service, behaviour, communication. A designer cannot "make" a brand; a designer
    builds the foundation the brand is perceived through.
- **A logo is not a magic lantern.** It cannot rescue a bad product or a poorly run organisation. A well-
  designed mark, repeated consistently, helps a good product reach its potential. The logo supplies
  attitude and direction; the organisation supplies the meaning, over time.
- **Meaning accrues.** Most marks are nearly empty when new; they become loaded through association and
  exposure. Simple geometric forms carry whatever the organisation pours into them — which also means a
  mark's meaning can be damaged by association. Choose forms that can hold the intended meaning for decades.
- **What a logo is for:** claim ownership and origin; differentiate from competitors; give the
  organisation an internal rallying point; let the audience form a relationship; lend credibility; bring
  order to many communications; create merchandising and recognition opportunities.

## 2. The twelve principles

These are synthesised from the whole body of logo-design practice. Treat them as a checklist of forces,
not as rigid laws — great marks sometimes break one principle deliberately to serve a stronger one.

### 1. Start with who, what, why
Before any form: *Who is the client (values, attitude, goals)? Who is the audience (demographic and
psychographic)? What is actually needed — is a logo even the answer, or is the problem a bad product,
message, or team?* Let the problem dictate the solution rather than imposing a favourite idea onto it. This
does not mean obeying every client whim, nor suppressing the designer's vision — it means gathering enough
information to set criteria, then creating through the designer's filter. Also ask what the organisation
wants to be in one, five and ten years; design for where it is going, not only where it is.

### 2. Identify, don't explain
Resist the urge to depict every facet of the business. Describing too much produces a cluttered mark and a
restraining identity that breaks the moment the business expands. The clearest, most direct voice wins.

### 3. Keep it simple
A simple form is recognised faster, remembered longer, reproduced more reliably, and survives abuse —
bad printing, embroidery, tiny favicons, careless in-house designers. Many logos fail from their own
cleverness or overproduction. Let something be what it is. (See §4: simple is not the same as plain.)

### 4. Make it relevant
The mark must fit the organisation, its industry, its audience and its personality. A florist's mark should
not look at home on a car dealership. Relevance is about *tone and fit*, not literal depiction (see §5).

### 5. Aim for distinction
Study what competitors look like. If every mark in the category runs together (all blue, all globes, all
swooshes), that is an opportunity. Bucking category conventions makes a brand stand out — but keep the
general perception in mind so the mark still reads as belonging to the right world.

### 6. Build memorability (mnemonic value)
Memory is built from shape and colour first, then context, then learned associations, then emotion (see §3).
A strong, singular silhouette and an ownable colour are the most reliable memory hooks.

### 7. Focus on one thing
One clear idea, executed well, beats three ideas fighting for attention. A mark with a single defining
feature is easier to recall and describe. If you cannot explain the idea in one sentence, it will not work.

### 8. Think small (and large)
Design for an inch, or 16 px. Intricate, layered forms that look superb at 400 % on screen become a
jumbled mess on a favicon or embroidered chest logo. Conversely, bad curves and sloppy kerning are
exaggerated on a building-sized sign. Test both extremes (see `testing-checklist.md`).

### 9. Design for longevity
Marks that date quickly come from concentrating on *formal* ideas (a trendy effect, the typeface of the
moment) rather than *conceptual* ones. Style and trend rarely carry lasting emotional resonance. Aim for a
mark that can convey its message for decades and adapt to cultural change.

### 10. Make the logo the foundation of a system
The audience never sees a logo in a void. It lives on cards, screens, vehicles, buildings, packaging,
next to photography and copy. Design the mark with its visual system in mind; the system should complement
the mark, not copy it. Guidelines protect the mark from party hats, wrong colours and floor decals.

### 11. Design for every medium
Assume the logo must work in one-colour print, on screen, as a 3D sign, embroidered, animated, and in media
not yet invented. Most clients only picture their current use; the designer's job is to plan for the
unplanned.

### 12. Be seductive — and be strong
Aesthetics matter. People would rather be drawn in by a mark than repelled by one; the message is primary but
the form must invite the viewer in. Simple, dynamic, well-crafted forms seduce. And "strong" applies to the
designer too: understand your role and the client's, keep a clear vision tied to the agreed goals, and
distinguish confidence from stubbornness. The designer is a catalyst for change; not everyone will be happy,
and that is not a failure.

**Two supporting principles worth naming separately:**
- **Pose a question (lightly).** A mark that lets the viewer complete a small puzzle — a hidden arrow, a
  letter doubling as an object, a figure/ground reversal — is looked at longer and remembered better. But
  there is a fine line: a mark that needs hours to decode or accompanying text will fail.
- **Respect tradition where it exists.** Existing equity (a colour, a shape, a heritage detail) and
  culturally significant symbols deserve respect. Borrowing a culture's sacred symbol requires permission
  from its custodians; honouring tradition can create powerful, unexpected connections.

## 3. The mnemonic model — how marks get remembered

People process a mark in roughly this order:

1. **Shape and colour.** All visual recognition starts here. Is it round and red? Square and green?
   Consistency of shape inside the mark (counters echoing the outer form, repeated angles) strengthens it.
2. **Historical continuity / context.** The viewer places the form in time and culture: contemporary,
   Victorian, retro-futurist, corporate, handmade. Signifiers of an era can intentionally evoke an attitude.
3. **Learned response.** Taught associations: red means stop, a green cross means pharmacy, a shield means
   protection. Use them knowingly; they differ by culture.
4. **Emotional resonance.** The "wild card": personal memories attached to colours and forms. Hard to predict,
   but it is where lasting attachment lives.

Design implication: get the silhouette and the colour decision right first; everything else is secondary.

## 4. Simplicity, properly understood

- **Less information, more memory.** The fewer words a phrase has, the easier it is to memorise; the fewer
  visual elements a mark has, the clearer and more memorable it is.
- **Simple is not plain.** Some shapes are too simple to be interesting — a bare circle or generic initials
  in a stock typeface carry nothing ownable. Simplicity charms only when it carries a certain amount of
  thought and detail: a clever junction, a precise proportion, an unexpected cut, a subtle light source.
- **Sculpt by subtraction.** Treat the process like sculpting: chip away weaker ideas, add and strip
  elements, then strip some more. Don't stop at the first acceptable result; the longer you stay with the
  form, the more correct it becomes.
- **The modernist vocabulary is shared ground.** Waves, stripes, stars, arrows, cubes, overlapping primitives
  with exclusions, half- and quarter-circles, spirals, repetition. Knowing this alphabet lets you compose on
  a solid foundation — but the vocabulary is shared, so push past its obvious combinations.
- **Minimalism as positioning.** Extreme reduction can itself be a differentiator — useful when a brand wants
  conceptual sophistication in a market of loud identities (common in B2B and premium sectors).

## 5. Relevance vs. literalness

- **Avoid direct illustration of the subject.** A dentist logo that is a tooth, a plumber logo that is a
  wrench, a software logo that is a laptop — these are forgettable because every competitor could use them.
- **Clichés are recognisable for a reason.** An apple, a shield, a leaf, a speech bubble — they read instantly.
  Don't discard them; present them in a fresh form so the familiar sign is seen anew.
- **Where to find the idea:** the name (a letter, a word meaning, a sound); the brand's adjectives (fast,
  warm, precise, rebellious); its promise; its origin, place or founder; a process or material it uses; an
  abstract phenomenon it creates (connection, growth, flow, balance).
- **Literal naming shortcuts.** When the name is itself an object (e.g. a company named after a fruit),
  using that object makes recall instant — the viewer sees the mark and the name surfaces automatically. That
  directness is a legitimate choice; add craft and a twist so it is still ownable.
- **Abstract marks need assigned meaning and exposure.** Some ideas ("connection", "speed") have strong visual
  analogues; others ("reliability", "trust") do not, so an abstract form gets meaning assigned by story and
  repetition. Abstract marks only become meaningful with a great deal of exposure — factor media budget into
  the choice.

## 6. Distinction and originality

- **The familiarity test.** If a solution feels familiar in even the slightest way and you know it isn't
  yours, it is someone else's. Discard it or change it significantly. Memory is sneaky: marks you saw years ago
  can resurface as "your" idea.
- **Basic shapes are common property; combinations are not.** Everyone can use circles and triangles; the
  specific arrangement is what must be new.
- **Search before you commit.** Check the category, trademark databases, and a reverse image search. Use the
  bundled library (`scripts/search_library.py`) to see what is already common for a subject or technique.
- **Never copy library marks.** The reference library exists to learn patterns and craft, not to trace.
  If an accidental near-copy is discovered after delivery, be honest, change it, and make it right.

## 7. Longevity vs. trend

- Every era leaves fingerprints (gradients, drop shadows, glossy bevels, ultra-thin type, lowercase
  everything). A trained eye can date a mark to its decade. Some brands deliberately want a current look — that
  is valid if chosen knowingly and budgeted for refresh.
- Two viable ownership strategies:
  - **Constancy**: one mark kept almost unchanged for generations; recognition compounds.
  - **Managed evolution**: periodic refreshes that keep the core recognisable (colour, shape, key element)
    while updating details.
- Custom letterforms add ownership, but the further they depart from recognisable letter shapes, the faster
  they date.
- Conceptual ideas outlive formal ones. Build the mark on an idea, not on an effect.

## 8. Tensions to manage

| Tension | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Simple ↔ interesting | Reduce until the idea is clear, then make sure one detail is ownable. |
| Relevant ↔ literal | Evoke the attitude; don't depict the product. |
| Distinct ↔ appropriate | Break category conventions where they blur together, keep the cues that signal the right world. |
| Clever ↔ clear | A small puzzle helps; a riddle hurts. One-sentence test. |
| Symmetry ↔ energy | Perfect symmetry is stable but can bore; a subtle asymmetry keeps the eye moving. |
| Consistency ↔ flexibility | A fixed core (shape, colour, type) with rules for variation keeps systems fresh without losing recognition. |
| Client taste ↔ strategy | Return to the brief and the agreed criteria, not to personal preference — yours or theirs. |
| Perfection ↔ progress | Keep refining details, but know that no logo pleases everyone; even a mark loved by 99 % will have critics. |

## 9. Quick diagnostic questions

Run these on any concept (yours or one you're critiquing):

1. Can I describe the idea in one sentence?
2. Is it recognisable as a silhouette, in one flat colour?
3. Does it still read at 16 px? At 16 m?
4. Would it survive a fax, an embroidery machine, a laser engraver?
5. Does it look like any competitor, or any famous mark?
6. Does its tone (sharp/round, heavy/light, warm/cool) match the brand's adjectives?
7. Is there one defining feature someone could describe from memory?
8. Is anything there only because it was fun to draw?
9. Will it look dated in ten years? Why not?
10. Does it work as the foundation of a system — can a pattern, icon set or motion idea grow from it?
11. Are there unintended readings (rotated, mirrored, at a distance, by someone who hasn't seen the brief)?
12. Would the audience — not the designer — understand and like it?
