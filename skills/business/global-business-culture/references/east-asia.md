# East Asia (China, Japan, Korea, Hong Kong/Taiwan/Singapore)

Schema: Snapshot / Communication / Trust / Decisions / Negotiation & contracts / Etiquette / Taboos / Variation / Field moves.

## China

**Snapshot.** Reactive culture (Lewis); high-context, hierarchical, relationship-based trust at maximum: business runs on **guanxi** (关系, reciprocal relationship networks) and **mianzi** (面子, face).

- **Communication.** Indirect; "maybe", "we will study it", or silence can mean no. Never force a public "no" — it costs face for both sides.
- **Trust.** Guanxi is the prerequisite, not the preliminary: reciprocal obligations built through shared meals, favors, and time. Contracts are trusted less than relationships because the legal system developed around relational rather than adversarial enforcement (documented in negotiation literature, e.g. Graham & Lam 2003; Pye 1992).
- **Decisions.** Slow consensus internally, then fast execution; the senior person in the room outranks the negotiating counterpart's title — map the real hierarchy first.
- **Negotiation & contracts.** Skilled, patient hagglers; initial offers leave wide room; a signed contract is traditionally a snapshot of the relationship and the beginning of cooperation — expect renegotiation when circumstances change. Do not treat the signature as the end of negotiation.
- **Etiquette.** Business cards two-handed with a moment's attention; banquet protocol is deal infrastructure (seating by rank, toasts, returning toasts; pulling out paperwork at the banquet signals bad faith); gifts: modest, given/received with both hands, not opened immediately; avoid clocks/umbrellas (funeral symbolism) and anything in sets of four.
- **Taboos.** Publicly correcting or embarrassing a counterpart; making someone lose face is a permanent injury; praising Taiwan as a country; political topics; drinking games refusal without a health excuse.
- **Variation.** State-owned enterprises: slower, more formal, party-committee parallel track. Tech private firms: faster, more Western. Hong Kong/Singapore: see below.
- **Field moves.** Invest in banquets as negotiations; use an intermediary for the first introduction; reciprocate every favor visibly; after signing, stay engaged — the relationship, not the paper, enforces the deal.

## Japan

**Snapshot.** The archetype reactive culture: harmony (和 wa), consensus decision-making, formalized etiquette. Decisions are made BEFORE the meeting, not in it.

- **Communication.** High-context + honne/tatemae (本音/建前): the public position vs. the real feeling. "That would be difficult" (ちょっと難しい) = no. "We will consider it positively" = probably no. Long pauses are processing, not objection.
- **Trust.** Long, slow, but extremely durable once built; loyalty to a supplier/partner survives price competition. JETRO's guidance for foreigners: meet one-on-one to hear honne off the record.
- **Decisions.** **Nemawashi** (根回し, "going around the roots"): informal one-on-one consensus-building before any formal proposal; **ringi** (稟議): the circulating stamped approval document that formalizes already-built consensus. Timelines of 2–6 months for significant commitments are normal, and multiple stakeholder meetings are a positive signal, not stalling. (JETRO; aqpartners.jp guide)
- **Negotiation & contracts.** Contracts are framework documents; the relationship and subsequent adjustments carry the load. Detailed Japanese-language materials your champion can circulate internally accelerate nemawashi.
- **Etiquette.** Cards (meishi) two-handed, received with care, placed on the table in seating order; senior person greeted/enters first; modest dress, modest self-presentation; gift-giving ritualized (quality, wrapped, from your region; given at the END of a first visit); after-work drinking (nomikai) is where honne surfaces.
- **Taboos.** Pushing for an immediate answer (yields a tatemae answer); blowing your stack — visible anger ends relationships; sticking chopsticks upright in rice; overdone physical contact/back-slapping.
- **Field moves.** Send agendas and materials well in advance; never surprise anyone in a meeting — if people would be surprised, you are not ready, do nemawashi first; confirm agreements in writing after; value the middle-aged section chief as much as the president.

## Korea

**Snapshot.** Confucian hierarchy + fast-moving economy; harmony (inhwa, 인화) and mood/sensitivity to face (**kibun**, 기분).

- **Communication.** Indirect among themselves, but negotiators can be surprisingly blunt and emotional with outsiders; silence common.
- **Trust.** Relationship-based; heavy after-hours socializing (hof/hoesik) builds the real channel; personal loyalty spans company changes.
- **Decisions.** Top-down but consultative within hierarchy; speed can be very fast once the top decides (chaebol style).
- **Negotiation & contracts.** Vigorous bargaining; contracts seen as baseline of an evolving relationship; changes requested post-signature are normal, not bad faith.
- **Etiquette.** Cards two-handed; bow + handshake hybrid; senior-first everything; drinking etiquette (pour for others, two hands, turn away from elders when drinking); gifts appreciated, given/received with both hands.
- **Taboos.** Writing a Korean name in red ink; publicly criticizing anyone senior; ignoring seating/hierarchy order; Japan-related topics handled with care.
- **Field moves.** Identify the number two — often the real negotiator; socialize deliberately; respect hierarchy visibly in every greeting order.

## Hong Kong, Taiwan, Singapore

**Hong Kong / Taiwan.** Chinese relationship DNA with faster, more transactional pace (HK finance-flavored); Taiwanese business culture is famously warm and relationship-dense; contracts still subordinate to relationships but more Western legal form in HK.

**Singapore.** The exception in the region: largely linear-active, low-corruption, contract-forward, English-language, precise; multi-ethnic etiquette awareness (Chinese majority; Malay and Indian norms for food/alcohol/greetings). Do not apply "flexible time" expectations to a Singaporean counterparty — it can read as disrespect.
