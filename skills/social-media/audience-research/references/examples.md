# Examples — audience profiles that actually help

Abbreviated profiles across business types, each contrasted with the **useless generic persona**.
Notice every one leads with jobs-to-be-done and real language, separates buyer vs follower where
relevant, and skips demographics that don't change the content. (Illustrative.)

---

## 1. B2B SaaS — payroll tool for small-business owners
- **Who:** owner-operators of 2–30-person businesses who do payroll themselves.
- **Buyer vs follower:** same person (the owner).
- **JTBD:** functional — "run payroll without messing up taxes"; emotional — "stop feeling
  anxious and out of my depth"; social — "look like I have my act together."
- **Pains:** terrified of the IRS; never sure it's right; it eats a whole evening.
- **Objections:** "switching sounds painful," "probably not built for someone like me."
- **Language bank:** "I don't even know where to start," "terrified I'll mess up taxes," "just
  want it off my plate." They say "the payroll stuff," not "payroll operations."
- *Generic persona (useless):* "Steve, 41, owns a business, $120k revenue, uses LinkedIn." → tells
  you nothing about what to post.

## 2. Local — neighborhood coffee shop
- **Who:** remote workers and students within ~3 miles looking for a third place.
- **JTBD:** functional — "a reliable spot to work for a few hours"; emotional — "feel at home,
  not like a customer"; social — "my regular place."
- **Pains:** sketchy wifi elsewhere; cafes that are weird about laptops; feeling anonymous.
- **Language bank:** "good wifi and they don't mind if you stay," "they know my order."
- *Generic persona:* "Coffee-lover Carla, 28, urban." → demographic theater.

## 3. Ecommerce / DTC — sustainable sneakers
- **Who:** design-conscious 25–40s who'd buy them on looks alone.
- **Buyer vs follower:** often the same; gift-buyers are a second segment.
- **JTBD:** functional — "shoes that look good and go with everything"; emotional — "feel good
  about the purchase without trying hard"; social — "be the person with the cool sustainable
  kicks."
- **Objections:** "sustainable usually means ugly or pricey," "will they actually be comfortable?"
- **Language bank:** "they actually look good," "didn't want to sacrifice style," "the ocean
  plastic thing is a bonus."
- *Generic persona:* "Eco-conscious millennial." → too broad to write to.

## 4. Personal brand — indie maker building in public
- **Who:** early/aspiring indie founders; "their people" are builders, not hustle-culture fans.
- **JTBD:** functional — "learn what actually works from someone real"; emotional — "feel less
  alone in the grind"; social — "be part of the build-in-public crowd."
- **Pains:** drowning in generic advice; impostor feelings; performative founder content.
- **Language bank:** "actual numbers, not vanity metrics," "tired of gurus," "show me the boring
  parts."
- *Generic persona:* "Tech-savvy entrepreneur, 30." → invisible.

## 5. Nonprofit — youth literacy (two audiences)
- **Segment A — supporters/donors:** JTBD: "give to something that visibly works"; pains:
  "donations vanish into overhead"; language: "I want to see the impact," "where does my money
  go."
- **Segment B — educators/partners:** JTBD: "get books into my kids' hands without a budget
  fight"; language: "my students have nothing to read at home."
- *Generic persona:* "Caring donor, 45+." → ignores that two very different audiences need
  different content.

---

## The pattern

- Lead with **jobs-to-be-done** (functional + emotional + social), not demographics.
- Capture the **real language** — the verbatim phrases — for copy to mirror.
- Separate **buyer vs follower** and **multiple audiences** where they differ.
- Include a trait only if it **changes the content**.
- The test: could a writer who never met them picture a real person and write something that
  makes that person feel seen? If yes, the profile works.
