# Positioning, Point of View & Proof

This is what makes content *magnetic* instead of merely competent. A brand with a clear
position and a real point of view is easy to write distinctive content for; a brand without
one produces interchangeable posts no matter how good the writing is.

## Positioning — three plain sentences

Get these in the brand's own words, specific and jargon-free:

1. **What it is:** "[Business] is a [category] that helps [audience] [outcome]."
2. **What's different:** the one thing it does that the obvious alternatives don't. Force a
   real answer — "cheaper/better/faster" is not a position. "The only one that ___" or
   "unlike [alternative], we ___" is.
3. **Why believe it:** the reason the difference is credible (see Proof below).

If the user can't name a difference, that's a finding, not a failure — capture "parity
category; differentiation is voice + consistency," which tells downstream skills to win on
personality and reliability rather than claims.

## Point of view — the beliefs that make content shareable

The single most under-captured asset. A point of view is a **stance the brand will defend**
— ideally one a reasonable person could disagree with. It's what turns a feed of updates
into a feed worth following.

Elicit it:

- "What does almost everyone in your space get wrong?"
- "What do you believe that your competitors would never say out loud?"
- "What advice do you give customers that they don't want to hear?"
- "What are you *against*?"

Capture 1–3 POVs the brand will stake out, and — just as important — note the takes it
*won't* make (off-limits, too risky, not authentic). Content skills use these to give posts a
spine; `contrarian-and-opinion` owns the craft of publishing them well (the narrowness test,
steelmanning, and the ragebait line).

> A brand that believes nothing in particular will sound like everyone. Find the conviction.

## Proof — the credible specifics

Vague authority ("trusted by industry leaders") persuades no one. Collect what a skeptic
would actually accept:

- **Numbers:** customers, revenue, results, percentages, time saved, growth.
- **Named customers / logos** (only those cleared to use publicly).
- **Outcomes / case results:** "cut onboarding from 3 weeks to 2 days."
- **Credentials / track record:** years, certifications, notable work, awards.
- **Social proof:** reviews, ratings, community size, testimonials.

Note any usage restrictions (which logos/figures are public, which need approval — and for
testimonials/UGC, **which permissions you actually hold**: organic reposting and paid-ad use
are different rights). Downstream skills will reach for these constantly; the more concrete,
the better the content. Two routing rules: numbers get verified before publishing
(`content-research-and-sourcing` — no stat ships unsourced), and testimonials/reviews follow
`social-proof-and-testimonials`' rules (FTC: real, typical, disclosed where required — never
fabricated or cherry-picked past honesty).

## Offers & CTAs — what we actually want people to do

Capture the real calls to action so content drives something:

- Primary CTA (sign up, book a demo, shop, subscribe, visit, donate).
- Secondary/soft CTAs (follow, comment, save, join the community, read more).
- Lead magnets / free tools / offers worth pointing to.
- Any "we never hard-sell on [platform]" rules.

## Competitor contrast (optional but powerful)

If relevant, capture how the brand wants to *sound and stand* relative to named competitors —
not to attack them, but to sharpen its own edges. "They're corporate and safe; we're direct
and a little irreverent." This contrast helps every content skill find the open lane.
