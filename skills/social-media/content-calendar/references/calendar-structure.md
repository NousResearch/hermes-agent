# Calendar Structure

Once cadence is set, design the **recurring skeleton** — the repeatable structure that means you're
never staring at a blank page. This is what a calendar *is*: not a list of posts, but a system that
generates them.

## Buckets, not specific posts

A content calendar defines **recurring slots** (buckets), each tied to a pillar and a format — not
individual captions. Example for a 4×/week LinkedIn + 3×/week TikTok founder:

- **Mon (LinkedIn):** teaching post from Pillar A (how-to)
- **Tue (TikTok):** storytime from Pillar B
- **Wed (LinkedIn):** contrarian POV from Pillar C
- **Thu (TikTok):** quick tip / listicle from Pillar A
- **Fri (LinkedIn):** behind-the-scenes / build-in-public from Pillar B
- **Fri (TikTok):** reactive/trend slot (left open)

Each slot is a **standing instruction**, not a fixed post. Filling a slot with this week's actual
post is the job of `batch-content-plan`. The skeleton is reusable every cycle.

## Map pillars × formats × slots

Build the grid from what's already decided:

- **Pillars** (from `content-pillars`) — what you talk about. Distribute slots across pillars so no
  theme dominates and your signature pillar gets prime real estate.
- **Formats** — how it's delivered (carousel, Reel/TikTok, thread, LinkedIn post, etc.), matched to
  the platform and the pillar.
- **Slots** — which day/platform. Spread formats so the feed has variety.

The grid guarantees pillar balance and format variety *by design*, instead of hoping for it.

## Recurring series / franchises

The strongest calendars run **named recurring series** — a repeatable franchise in a fixed slot:

- "Teardown Tuesday" (you critique something each week)
- "#FixItFriday" (one common mistake + the fix)
- "Build Log" (weekly build-in-public update)

Why they work: **anticipation** (audiences return for the next one), **less decision fatigue** (the
format's decided — just fill it), and **brand recognition** (the series becomes a thing you're known
for). Aim for one or two franchises; don't make every slot a series.

## Planned vs reactive — leave room

A calendar that's 100% pre-planned can't ride a trend, react to news, or jump on a moment — and
those are often the highest-reach posts. Reserve capacity for **reactive content**:

- A rough split: **~70–80% planned/evergreen, ~20–30% reactive/timely.**
- Keep one or two **open slots** per week explicitly for trend-jacking, news, or community moments
  (hand the live trend research to `trend-jacking`).

Structure gives you consistency; the reactive room gives you relevance. You need both.

## Anchor known dates

Layer time-bound anchors onto the recurring skeleton:

- **Launches / announcements** — work backward from the date with teaser → launch → follow-up.
- **Seasonal / industry events** — conferences, seasonal moments, recurring industry dates relevant
  to *your* audience (not every generic holiday).
- **Campaigns / series arcs** — a multi-week themed run.

Anchors are the exception layer on top of the steady recurring rhythm — not a replacement for it.

## The output

A content calendar deliverable is: the **cadence** (per platform), the **recurring skeleton**
(buckets by pillar × format × slot), the **series**, the **planned/reactive split**, and any
**anchors** — plus a filled **sample week** to make it concrete. That's a reusable system, not a
disposable list.
