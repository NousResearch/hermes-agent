# The Voice Analysis Framework

This is how you reverse-engineer a voice from writing — the difference between this skill and
every "describe your brand voice" tool. Work the six layers in order. For each, you're doing
three things: **observing** specific features, **citing** the evidence (quote the sample), and
**translating** the observation into a reproducible rule.

> Rule of the whole framework: *no observation without evidence.* If you can't point to a line
> in a sample, you're guessing — and guesses are what make AI content feel generic.

A fast way to surface patterns: read 2–3 samples and underline anything you could imagine the
writer doing *again*. Recurring choices are voice; one-offs are noise.

---

## Layer 1 — Lexicon (the words)

What words they reach for is the most immediate voice signal.

**Observe:**
- **Register** — Anglo-Saxon and plain ("use," "buy," "help") vs Latinate and formal
  ("utilize," "purchase," "facilitate"). Most distinctive voices skew plain.
- **Contractions** — "don't / you're / it's" vs "do not / you are / it is." A single biggest
  formality tell. Note their default and any exceptions.
- **Signature words & phrases** — words they reach for repeatedly; their name for the product,
  the customer, the category ("members" vs "users"; "ship" vs "launch"). Pet phrases.
- **Concreteness** — abstract ("solutions," "value," "experiences") vs concrete and sensory
  ("a spreadsheet that emails you at 2am"). Concrete is usually the better voice.
- **Intensifiers & hedges** — "very, really, honestly, literally" vs "kind of, maybe, I think,
  sort of." The ratio reveals confidence.
- **Jargon level** — how much insider language, and whether it's used to include or to flex.
- **Slang / profanity** — present? what kind? how strong? This is a hard guardrail to capture.

**Translate to a rule:** "Plain Anglo-Saxon vocabulary; always contracts; calls them
'builders' not 'users'; bans 'solution,' 'leverage,' 'utilize'; one concrete example per post."

---

## Layer 2 — Syntax (how sentences are built)

Rhythm is voice you can feel before you can name. It lives in sentence construction.

**Observe:**
- **Sentence length & variance** — uniform medium sentences read as corporate. Distinctive
  voices vary: a string of short punches, then one long flowing sentence. Measure roughly
  (short ≈ <10 words, long ≈ >25) and note the *pattern*, not just the average.
- **Openers** — do sentences/posts start with "I," with "And/But," with a question, with a
  number, with the subject? Recurring openers are a strong signature.
- **Fragments** — do they use them on purpose? "No fluff. Just the work." Deliberate fragments
  are a major voice marker.
- **Active vs passive** — "we shipped it" vs "it was shipped." Most strong voices are active.
- **Parallelism & triads** — do they build in threes? List in rhythm? "Faster, cheaper,
  yours."
- **Clause complexity** — simple declaratives vs subordinate-clause-heavy, comma-spliced flow.
- **Punctuation as rhythm** — em-dashes for interruption, colons for setup-payoff, ellipses
  for trailing, periods for staccato. Note their default rhythm tools.

**Translate to a rule:** "Short declaratives, 6–12 words, broken by one long sentence per
paragraph. Frequent fragments for emphasis. Opens many lines with 'And' or 'But.' Em-dashes,
never semicolons."

---

## Layer 3 — Rhetoric (the moves)

The recurring devices that make a point land their way.

**Observe:**
- **Rhetorical questions** — do they ask to set up an answer? How often?
- **Direct address** — how much "you"? Intimate and conversational, or detached third-person?
- **Repetition / anaphora** — repeated openings or phrases for rhythm and emphasis.
- **Analogy & metaphor** — frequency and *type*. Sports? cooking? engineering? pop culture? The
  metaphor domain is itself a fingerprint.
- **Contrast / antithesis** — "not X, but Y." "Most people Z. The best people W." A workhorse
  of opinionated content; note if they lean on it.
- **Assertion vs story** — do they state a claim, or tell a small story to imply it?
- **Humor** — type (dry, self-deprecating, absurd, deadpan, none) and target (themselves, the
  category, never the customer). Capture the *kind*, and the lines it won't cross.
- **The turn** — how they pivot or land the point: a callback, a one-line mic-drop, a question,
  a reframe. Most strong writers have a signature landing.

**Translate to a rule:** "Self-deprecating dry humor, never at the customer's expense. Builds
arguments by contrast ('everyone does X; do Y instead'). Lands posts with a short reframe, not
a CTA."

---

## Layer 4 — Structure (how a piece is organized)

Zoom out from the sentence to the whole post.

**Observe:**
- **Opening move** — hook style: provocation, question, stat, confession, in-media-res story.
- **Build** — linear argument, layered (claim → evidence → implication), listicle, or narrative.
- **Close** — CTA, callback to the open, mic-drop line, soft fade, question. Note the default.
- **Paragraphing & whitespace** — one-line paragraphs? dense blocks? The visual rhythm on the
  feed is part of voice.
- **Formatting habits** — bold, lists, the "hook → blank line → body" LinkedIn pattern, emoji
  as bullets, arrows. Capture the recurring scaffolding.

**Translate to a rule:** "Opens with a one-line provocation, blank line, then 3–4 short
paragraphs building by example, closes on a question. Heavy whitespace; no lists."

---

## Layer 5 — Stance (relationship and attitude)

The hardest layer to fake and the easiest to feel. It's the *posture* toward the reader.

**Observe:**
- **Distance** — intimate ("between us…") vs formal ("one might consider"). Peer or podium?
- **Authority posture** — peer ("I'm figuring this out too"), mentor ("here's what I learned"),
  expert ("here's how it works"), or provocateur ("you're doing it wrong").
- **Confidence** — assertive and unhedged vs careful and qualified.
- **Emotional register** — warm, cool, intense, playful, earnest. The temperature.
- **Self-disclosure** — how much do they reveal? Vulnerable, or guarded?
- **For / against** — what they champion and what they push against. Strong voices have enemies
  (ideas, not people).

**Translate to a rule:** "Peer-to-peer, lightly authoritative — 'here's what worked for me,'
never 'you must.' Warm but unsentimental. Shares failures openly; keeps private life off-limits.
Against hustle-culture theater."

---

## Layer 6 — Negative space (what they never do)

Often the most defining layer, and the one everyone skips. A voice is shaped as much by its
omissions as its habits.

**Observe what's conspicuously absent:**
- Punctuation they never use (no exclamation marks? no emoji? no semicolons?).
- Words/constructions they avoid (no buzzwords? no superlatives? never "I'm excited to
  announce"?).
- Tones they won't strike (never salesy? never preachy? never cutesy?).
- Moves they won't make (never humble-brags? never dunks on people? never uses hashtags?).

**Translate to hard "never"s:** these become guardrails every downstream skill must respect.
"Never uses exclamation marks. Never opens with 'I'm thrilled.' Never more than one emoji.
Never dunks on a named competitor."

---

## From layers to fingerprint

After working all six layers, you'll have dozens of observations. Distill to the **5–7 most
distinctive, reproducible signatures** — the ones that carry the most recognition per word.
Test each candidate: *if a post broke this rule, would it feel off?* If yes, it's fingerprint
material. If it's true but generic ("uses clear language"), cut it.

A strong fingerprint reads like a recipe a stranger could follow — and like a fingerprint, it
should be unique enough to identify the writer in a lineup.
