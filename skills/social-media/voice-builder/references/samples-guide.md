# Samples Guide — sourcing the right writing

The analysis is only as good as the samples. Garbage in, generic out. This guide gets you
material that actually reveals the voice.

## How many

- **Ideal:** 5–10 substantial samples.
- **Floor:** 3. Below that, proceed but mark the voice guide low-confidence and name the gaps.
- **Ghostwriting at scale:** lean toward 10+, spanning the formats you'll write for.

More isn't always better — three samples that are unmistakably *them* beat fifteen that are
inconsistent or committee-edited.

## What makes a good sample

- **Actually written by the person.** Not ghostwritten by someone else, not legal/marketing
  boilerplate, not AI-generated. You're capturing *their* voice.
- **Something they're proud of / feels like them.** Ask: "Show me 3 things you've written that
  felt the most *you*." Their judgment is a useful signal.
- **In a natural register.** Off-the-cuff often beats polished — less self-editing, more voice.
- **Relevant format.** If you'll write LinkedIn posts, weight LinkedIn samples. But mix in
  other formats too; the through-line across formats *is* the voice.

## The best source most people forget: spoken transcripts

Transcripts of the person **talking** — a podcast, a webinar, a voice memo, a Loom, a sales
call — are gold. People's spoken voice is their most natural; writing tends to formalize it.
A 5-minute voice memo answering "why did you start this?" often reveals more voice than ten
polished posts. Ask for one.

Note the caveat: spoken language has filler ("um," "like," false starts) and run-ons. Capture
the *rhythm and word choice*; record a cleanup rule for the written version (e.g., "keep the
conversational openers, cut the filler, break run-ons into short sentences").

## Sample types, ranked roughly by signal

1. Spoken transcripts (most natural voice).
2. Personal posts / threads they wrote themselves.
3. Replies, comments, DMs (unguarded, conversational).
4. Newsletters / blog posts (more considered, still them).
5. Marketing/website copy (often committee voice — use cautiously).

## What to exclude

- Anything ghostwritten or heavily edited by others.
- Legal, compliance, or boilerplate copy.
- AI-generated drafts.
- Clear outliers (a one-off formal piece that isn't representative).
- Old material if the voice has since evolved (ask what's current).

## When samples conflict (voice drift)

If samples disagree — formal in one place, loose in another — don't average them. Surface it:
"Your newsletter sounds buttoned-up; your X posts are loose and funny. Which is the voice you
want everywhere — or do they stay different by channel?" Then anchor on the target, and capture
the channel split as tone variation, not a contradiction.

## Aspirational voice

If the person wants to sound *different* than they currently do, collect a second set:
**samples of writers/brands they want to sound like**. Label them clearly as aspirational. In
the analysis, separate "your current voice" from "your target voice," and be transparent about
what's being borrowed so the result still feels honest and ownable — not an impersonation.

## Quick intake script

If the user doesn't know what to share, ask for:
1. "The 3 things you've written that felt the most *you*."
2. "A voice memo or transcript of you explaining what you do, like you'd tell a friend."
3. "A reply or DM where you were just being yourself."

That's usually enough to start.
